Why these two plants?

Despite belonging to entirely different plant families and growing on different continents, P. niruri and C. sativa share a surprising degree of phytochemical overlap — particularly in the flavonoid and monoterpene classes that drive their anti-inflammatory properties.

Phyllanthaceae
Phyllanthus niruri
Chanca piedra · Gale of the wind · Stone-breaker
  • Pantropical weed; common seasonal herb in East African highlands including Kirinyaga County
  • Traditional uses: hepatitis B, kidney stones, malaria, diabetes, inflammation
  • Key compound classes: lignans, hydrolysable tannins, flavonoids, triterpenes
  • Standardisation marker: phyllanthin (lignan); 0.5–1% in commercial extracts
  • Aqueous LD₅₀ > 5,000 mg/kg in rat models — classified non-toxic
Cannabaceae
Cannabis sativa
Hemp · Marijuana (medical cultivars)
  • Cultivated globally; pharmacological interest centres on cannabinoid and terpene content
  • Key compound classes: cannabinoids (THC/CBD), 120+ terpenes across 5 structural classes, flavonoids
  • Distinctive flavonoids: canniflavins A/B/C — structurally unique prenylated flavones with COX inhibition
  • β-Caryophyllene: only terpene that acts as a cannabinoid; CB2 receptor agonist
  • Regulatory status highly variable — jurisdiction-dependent
The structural convergence argument: When two phylogenetically distant plants independently produce the same molecule — rutin, quercetin, astragalin, kaempferol, limonene, p-cymene — that convergence is not coincidence. It reflects evolutionary pressure toward compounds that modulate inflammation, oxidative stress, and microbial defence through conserved biochemical pathways. For researchers and clinicians, shared chemistry implies shared mechanism.

Terpene map

Terpenes are volatile secondary metabolites built from isoprene units (C5). They account for the aroma profiles of both plants and contribute significantly to anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and penetration-enhancing activities.

Both plants share limonene (C10 monoterpene) and p-cymene (C10 monoterpene) by confirmed chemical identity. C. sativa carries 120 terpenes across 5 structural classes; P. niruri is narrower — monoterpenes and triterpenes are the documented classes, with sesquiterpenes present across the Phyllanthus genus.

P. niruri — documented terpenes
Limonene Monoterpene (C10). Anti-carcinogenic in liver tumour models; skin penetration enhancer (diclofenac uptake); antibacterial. One of two shared monoterpenes.
p-Cymene Monoterpene (C10). Antioxidant; antimicrobial; prevents α-tocopherol (vitamin E) oxidation in formulations. Identical molecule in both plants.
Lupeol Triterpene (C30, lupane-type). Skin chemopreventive; hepatoprotective; apoptotic in human pancreatic adenocarcinoma cell lines. P. niruri-exclusive — no cannabis equivalent.
Phyllanthenol Triterpene long-chain terpenoid alcohol. Antioxidant contribution; co-occurs with lupeol in root/stem fractions.
Phyllantheol / phyllanthenone Triterpene variants. Documented across aerial parts; structurally related to lupeol class.
C. sativa — 120 terpenes (selected)
Limonene Monoterpene. Present across 61 cannabis monoterpenes. Citrus aroma; anxiolytic, antibacterial. Shared with P. niruri by identity.
p-Cymene Monoterpene. Antioxidant synergist in the terpene blend. Isomeric to thyme-derived cymene. Shared with P. niruri by identity.
β-Myrcene Monoterpene. Sedative; analgesic; anti-inflammatory. Among the most abundant cannabis monoterpenes. No equivalent in P. niruri.
β-Caryophyllene Sesquiterpene (C15). CB2 receptor agonist — the only terpene that functions as a cannabinoid. Anti-inflammatory via endocannabinoid system.
α-Pinene / Linalool Monoterpenes. α-Pinene: bronchodilatory, memory-supporting (AChE inhibition). Linalool: anxiolytic, sedative.
51 sesquiterpenes (C15) Largest aromatic class in cannabis. Includes humulene, bisabolol, farnesene. Anti-inflammatory; characteristic cannabis aroma.
2 triterpenes (C30) Friedelin and epifriedelanol — friedelan-type skeleton. Structurally distinct from P. niruri's lupane-type lupeol. Activities not equivalent.
Structural classes compared
Monoterpenes (C10) Both plants: limonene + p-cymene confirmed by identity. 2 isoprene units. Most volatile class — responsible for aroma and topical penetration enhancement. Cannabis has 61 monoterpenes total; P. niruri's monoterpene profile is narrower but includes the two shared molecules.
Sesquiterpenes (C15) Cannabis: 51 compounds — dominant aromatic class. P. niruri: sesquiterpenes documented for Phyllanthus genus (~40 species-level), but species-specific list for P. niruri is not well characterised in reviewed literature. No direct overlap claimed.
Diterpenes (C20) Cannabis: 2 confirmed (phytol class). P. niruri: phytol-class possible but not reported in core literature for this species.
Triterpenes (C30) P. niruri: lupeol, phyllanthenol, phyllantheol — well-documented, bioactive, lupane-type skeleton. Cannabis: 2 triterpenes (friedelan-type) — different structural class. No bioactivity equivalence should be assumed.

Flavonoid overlap

Flavonoids are polyphenolic secondary metabolites with well-characterised anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory mechanisms. The degree of flavonoid convergence between these two unrelated plant families is pharmacologically significant.

At minimum 5 flavonoids are confirmed shared by identity: rutin, quercetin, astragalin (kaempferol-3-O-glucoside), vitexin/orientin, and apigenin. This is a high degree of convergence for plants in the Phyllanthaceae and Cannabaceae families, which last shared a common ancestor hundreds of millions of years ago.

Structural analogy (not identity)
Nirurin (P. niruri) and Canniflavins (C. sativa) are both prenylated flavonoids — same class, different backbone oxidation state. Structural analogy suggests parallel anti-inflammatory activity, but activities are not directly transferable without experimental confirmation.

Bioactivity bridge

Where compounds are chemically identical across both plants, documented bioactivity in one plant applies directly to the other. Where compounds are structurally analogous (same class, different glycoside), activity is plausible but requires independent confirmation.

Equivalence symbols used below: ≡ = identical molecule, activity transfers directly · ~ = same aglycone, different sugar (activity plausible, not confirmed for glycoside) · ≈ = structural analogy, parallel mechanism inferred · ≠ = no structural equivalent in other plant

Strong overlap

Antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial — carried by shared quercetin, rutin, limonene, and p-cymene. Activity documented independently in both plants; mechanism identical at the molecular level.

Moderate overlap

Immunomodulation (kaempferol skeleton), anti-cancer (quercetin, limonene), capillary and vascular support (rutin). Shared mechanisms with plant-specific glycoside variations.

P. niruri exclusive advantage

Hepatoprotection, anti-lithic (kidney stone dissolution), anti-HBV, anti-HIV in vitro — driven by unique lignans (phyllanthin), lupeol, and hydrolysable tannins (geraniin, ellagic acid). No cannabis equivalent for these compound classes.

C. sativa exclusive advantage

CB2 agonism via β-caryophyllene (endocannabinoid system modulation), 120-compound terpene breadth, canniflavin COX inhibition, and clinically-studied cannabinoid (CBD/THC) pharmacology.

Preparation protocol — P. niruri

Different compound classes require different extraction methods. A single preparation cannot capture all bioactive fractions simultaneously. Three distinct preparations are described below, each targeting a specific compound class.

Extraction targets: (A) Aqueous decoction — flavonoids, tannins, lignans · (B) Hydroalcoholic extract — broadest phytochemical range · (C) Essential oil — monoterpenes (limonene, p-cymene) · (D) Lipophilic fraction — lupeol and triterpenes. These require separate, sequential preparations.
A Aqueous decoction — traditional, field-appropriate
1
Plant material
Harvest whole aerial parts at pre-flowering stage for maximum phytochemical content. In Kirinyaga County, P. niruri is a seasonal weed in humid, shaded borders. Wash thoroughly. Sun-dry on raised racks 3–5 days, or shade-dry 7–10 days. Grind to coarse powder. Target yield: ~1,800 g dried powder per extraction batch.
2
Decoction
Boil 4–8 g dried powder in 300 ml water for 10–15 minutes at atmospheric pressure. Strain through muslin or fine cloth. Cool before use. This captures: flavonoids (rutin, quercetin, astragalin), hydrolysable tannins (geraniin, corilagin, ellagic acid), lignans (partially). Water solubility confirmed at 26.2% — highest of all tested extraction solvents.
Monoterpenes (limonene, p-cymene) are largely lost at boiling — use Preparation C for terpene-targeted extraction.
3
Dosing — decoction
Standard: 2 cups (~300 ml each) daily, taken away from meals. Equivalent to ~8–16 g dried herb/day. Consistent with Ayurvedic and East African traditional practice. Aqueous LD₅₀ in rats: >5,000 mg/kg body weight — classified non-toxic in animal models. Recommended duration for hepatoprotective / anti-lithic applications: 4–8 weeks.
B Hydroalcoholic extract — optimal phytochemical breadth
1
Solvent selection
50% ethanol (v/v) in water — documented optimal for P. niruri for total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity. Captures both hydrophilic (flavonoids, tannins) and moderately lipophilic (quercetin aglycone, lignans, p-cymene-related fractions) simultaneously.
2
Maceration
Cold maceration: soak dried powder in 50% ethanol at 1:10 w/v ratio, 24–72 hours at room temperature with periodic agitation. Filter through Whatman No. 1 paper. Evaporate ethanol under reduced pressure at <40°C, or use as tincture directly. Soxhlet at 75°C gives more exhaustive extraction but risks degrading heat-sensitive phenolics.
3
Standardisation target
For research or commercial preparations: standardise to 0.5–1% phyllanthin (lignan marker). Dose: 150–300 mg standardised extract twice daily. Ethanol extract LD₅₀: non-toxic up to 300 mg/kg in Sprague-Dawley rats (subchronic); no genotoxic effects documented at 50–250 mg/kg over 30 days.
C Essential oil fraction — terpene-targeted (limonene + p-cymene)
1
Steam distillation
Use fresh aerial parts — limonene and p-cymene are volatile and partially lost during drying. Steam distil fresh material in a Clevenger apparatus. Collect essential oil layer. Yield is low (P. niruri is not an oil-rich plant), but both monoterpenes are confirmed by GC-MS in published studies.
2
Application — topical penetration enhancement
Limonene is documented to enhance transdermal diclofenac penetration in hairless rat models — relevant for topical wound and anti-inflammatory formulations. Dilute essential oil to 1–5% in carrier oil (coconut, jojoba) before topical application. p-Cymene at low concentrations functions as antioxidant synergist in the formulation.
Do not apply undiluted essential oil to skin.
D Triterpene fraction — lupeol (hepatoprotective / skin chemopreventive)
1
Non-polar extraction
Lupeol and phyllanthenol are non-polar — extracted with petroleum ether or n-hexane in a Soxhlet apparatus. Sequential extraction (petroleum ether → ethanol → water) yields three distinct fractions, each enriched for a different compound class. The non-polar fraction is lupeol-rich.
2
Bioactivity and use context
Lupeol (lupane-type pentacyclic triterpene, C30): skin chemopreventive — suppresses benzoyl peroxide-induced cutaneous toxicity; hepatoprotective against cadmium-induced liver toxicity; apoptotic in human pancreatic adenocarcinoma cell lines in vitro. This is the most differentiated advantage of P. niruri in any combined formulation — cannabis triterpenes are friedelan-type and structurally distinct, with no equivalent activity.

For patients — autoimmune and type 2 inflammatory conditions

This section translates the phytochemical data above into plain-language relevance for people living with autoimmune conditions or chronic type 2 (low-grade) inflammation. It does not constitute medical advice.

What is type 2 inflammation?

Type 2 inflammation is driven primarily by Th2 immune cells, IgE antibodies, and cytokines including IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13. It underlies conditions such as atopic dermatitis (eczema), allergic asthma, food allergies, and eosinophilic disorders. It is distinct from the type 1 / Th1 response that dominates in conditions like rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease, and multiple sclerosis — although mixed-type inflammation is common. Many conventional pharmaceuticals target single pathways; botanical compounds often act on multiple convergent pathways simultaneously.

🔴Atopic dermatitis / eczema
  • Astragalin (shared): anti-IgE and anti-dermatitis activity in preclinical models — inhibits mast cell degranulation
  • Quercetin (shared): mast cell stabiliser; reduces histamine release in cell models
  • Lupeol (P. niruri only): suppresses cutaneous inflammatory response to chemical irritants in rodent skin models
  • β-Caryophyllene (cannabis only): CB2 agonism modulates keratinocyte inflammation
  • Limonene (shared): topical penetration enhancer — could increase delivery of co-formulated compounds through skin barrier
🫁Allergic asthma
  • Quercetin (shared): reduces bronchial hyperresponsiveness in animal models via inhibition of type 2 cytokines
  • α-Pinene (cannabis only): documented bronchodilatory effect; AChE inhibitor
  • Apigenin (shared): anti-inflammatory flavone with potential airway-protective properties in cell models
  • Rutin (shared): capillary protective — may reduce oedema in airway mucosa
⚙️Rheumatoid arthritis (type 1/mixed)
  • Quercetin (shared): inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) in synovial cell models
  • Canniflavin A/B (cannabis only): COX inhibition — same pathway as NSAIDs, with lower gastric side-effect profile in early studies
  • Phyllanthin / hypophyllanthin (P. niruri only): anti-inflammatory in carrageenan-induced paw oedema models
  • β-Caryophyllene (cannabis only): CB2 agonism in joint tissues; anti-nociceptive in preclinical pain models
🧬Autoimmune hepatitis / liver inflammation
  • Phyllanthin / hypophyllanthin (P. niruri only): hepatoprotective against paracetamol-induced and carbon tetrachloride-induced hepatotoxicity in rodent models
  • Lupeol (P. niruri only): hepatoprotective against cadmium toxicity; modulates liver enzymes in animal studies
  • Geraniin / corilagin (P. niruri only): antiviral against HBV in cell culture — relevant for hepatitis B-associated liver inflammation
  • Ellagic acid (P. niruri only): hepatoprotective, anti-carcinogenic; DNA protective
🫀Metabolic inflammation (type 2)
  • Quercetin (shared): documented insulin-sensitising and anti-glycation effects in cell models; anti-inflammatory in adipose tissue
  • Kaempferol (shared): inhibits adipogenesis in cell models; antioxidant relevant to oxidative stress in metabolic syndrome
  • Rutin (shared): improves vascular integrity; anti-hypertensive in rodent models
  • Phyllanthin (P. niruri only): anti-diabetic effects documented in streptozotocin-induced rat models
💡Oxidative stress (cross-condition)
  • p-Cymene (shared): prevents α-tocopherol oxidation — antioxidant synergist in multi-compound extracts
  • Ellagic acid (P. niruri only): free-radical scavenging; DNA protection in genotoxicity assays
  • Quercetin / rutin (shared): among the most well-characterised antioxidant flavonoids; reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging at nM concentrations in vitro
  • Isoquercitrin (P. niruri only): potent antioxidant in DPPH and ORAC assays
Key concept — why compound convergence matters

Chronic inflammation rarely has a single molecular target. Most inflammatory conditions involve multiple dysregulated pathways operating simultaneously — NF-κB, COX, lipoxygenase, mast cell signalling, Th2 cytokine cascades, and reactive oxygen species. A botanical extract with 5 shared flavonoids + 2 shared terpenes + unique class-specific compounds (lignans, lupeol) acts across several of these pathways at once.

This polypharmacological profile is both a strength (broader coverage) and a research challenge (difficult to attribute effect to a single compound). It is one reason why standardised extracts — where phyllanthin content is controlled — are preferred in clinical research over raw herbal preparations.

Before using any botanical preparation: The evidence base for P. niruri and cannabis-derived products ranges from well-replicated in vitro findings to limited clinical data. Most studies cited here are cell models or animal studies — these do not automatically predict human efficacy or safety. Interactions with immunosuppressants, anticoagulants (quercetin may potentiate warfarin), and HIV/hepatitis medications are possible. Always discuss with a qualified clinician before beginning any botanical protocol, particularly if you are managing an autoimmune condition or taking prescription medication.
